Finding Movement You Enjoy Is How You Stay Capable for Life

By Jake Murillo · November 27, 2026

It’s Black Friday morning, and I’m at the park, not outside Best Buy or Target.

The air is thin, the weather’s cold, and there’s a quiet calm that comes with being outside before the sun rises.

Other years, I might’ve been standing outside Best Buy or Target, bundled up against the cold, watching a line of people stretch around the building, all of us waiting for those doors to open.

And if you remember those Black Friday mornings, you know exactly what I’m talking about.

Back when getting a good deal meant waking up before sunrise, flipping through store ads, and standing shoulder to shoulder with complete strangers, hoping whatever you came for wouldn’t sell out before you got through the doors.

Yeah, I’m part of that strange generation caught somewhere between millennials and Gen Z. Old enough to remember when Black Friday was practically a competitive sport, but young enough to appreciate that nowadays you can do all that shopping from your phone without ever leaving your bed.

Funny how times change.

These days, though, I’d rather be outside watching the sun come up than standing in line waiting for a store to open.

But what I know now is this: when life gets loud and it’s easy to avoid movement, you need something you actually enjoy doing. Something that’ll get you up and moving even when it’s cold, hot, or inconvenient.

Because you’re not going to move the same way forever.

And that’s exactly why you should make the most of your ability to move today, tomorrow, and for as long as you can.

You Don’t Have to Be Running to Be Moving

One thing I’ve learned about myself is that I genuinely enjoy being outside.

Cold mornings. Hot mornings. Before sunrise. Sometimes when the rest of the world is still asleep.

It’s peaceful.

Not every time I’m out running am I really running.

More often than not, I’m just moving.

And that counts.

That’s what sustainable fitness can look like.

Too many people wait for a “real workout” or punish themselves with something they hate, then wonder why they can’t stick with it.

A walk counts.

A hike counts.

A bike ride with your kid counts.

Shooting hoops at the park counts.

It’s not soft. It’s smart.

And there’s research supporting the importance of finding physical activity you actually enjoy.

In a 2024 randomized trial, people coached to exercise at an intensity that felt pleasant attended the gym 77% more often over eight weeks than those following a standard approach.

And a 2025 study examining a 15-week outdoor exercise program in parks found that people who felt better about the movement itself were more likely to keep showing up.

Think about that.

Enjoying what you’re doing can make a meaningful difference in whether you continue doing it.

You don’t have to force yourself into a version of exercise you dread.

You need to find something you’ll actually do.

We Were Created to Move, Not Sit All Day

I’m a firm believer that we were created by the Creator with intention.

And part of that intention, I believe, was for us to move and be active.

Look at what the human body is capable of.

We can run, climb, swim, jump, lift, and explore.

Yet somehow, we’ve created lifestyles where it’s easier than ever to avoid doing those things.

We drive to work.

We sit at work.

We drive home.

Then we sit on the couch because we’re exhausted.

From what?

Sitting all day?

Funny how that works, isn’t it?

Now, I’m not saying there’s anything wrong with sitting down to enjoy a movie or spending an evening relaxing.

Rest matters.

But there’s a difference between resting and allowing inactivity to become your lifestyle.

A 2024 research review examining sedentary behavior and cardiometabolic health found evidence linking more leisure-time sitting with increased risks of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, including coronary artery disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes.

That doesn’t mean you need to panic about every hour you spend on the couch.

But it does mean your daily habits deserve some attention.

Life makes sitting easy.

That doesn’t make prolonged inactivity harmless or inevitable.

And this isn’t just about you.

What About the Life You Say You Want?

Think about your friends.

Your family.

The people you love and the experiences you want to share with them.

If all you practice is eating and drinking, you’ll get good at eating and drinking.

But what about the life you say you want?

The trips.

The tours.

The adventures.

The unexpected climb you didn’t plan for.

You don’t want to find out halfway through your dream vacation that your body can’t keep up with the experiences you’ve been looking forward to.

Think about standing at the base of Enchanted Rock, that enormous granite dome rising up in front of you.

Sure, someone can show you pictures.

You could watch a video.

You could even follow someone else’s point of view through Meta glasses.

But none of that compares to feeling your legs working against the incline, your breathing getting heavier, and the view widening as you climb higher until you’re standing at the top.

You can watch someone else experience the world, or you can prepare your body to experience it yourself.

And if you have the ability to do the latter, why settle for the former?

Now, I’m not saying everyone has the same physical abilities. Injuries, disabilities, illnesses, and aging can change what we’re capable of doing, and sometimes assistance or accessibility accommodations are essential.

But if you’re capable of maintaining or improving your physical independence, why wouldn’t you make that a priority?

Why voluntarily place limitations on yourself through inactivity?

Because fitness isn’t just about what you see in the mirror.

It’s about what your body allows you to experience when you step away from it.

I Learned This Young, and I Credit My Mom

Thankfully, I learned to appreciate movement at a young age.

And I credit a lot of that to my mom.

She got me outside.

She took me to parks, let me play in the grass and dirt, and encouraged me to be active.

At the time, I wasn’t thinking about exercise science, cardiovascular health, or burning calories.

I was just a kid having fun.

And looking back, those experiences meant more than I realized.

Shout out to you, Mom. I love you for that.

My dad wasn’t necessarily big on structured exercise, but we still found ways to get outside.

We’d play basketball.

Ride bikes.

Go fishing.

And spend time together.

It all counted.

Not because we were following some elaborate fitness program, but because we were moving and making memories.

And if you didn’t grow up that way, that’s okay.

You can still start now.

You don’t need to have played sports your entire life.

You don’t need to be an athlete.

You just need to find something you enjoy enough to keep doing.

Stop Waiting for the Perfect Time

After more than a decade working as a personal trainer, I’ve heard just about every version of the same promise.

“I’ll start next week.”

“I’ll get back into it after the holidays.”

“I just need to find the motivation.”

Or my personal favorite:

“When things slow down.”

Well, I’ve got news for you.

Life doesn’t necessarily slow down.

There’s always another responsibility, another appointment, another holiday, or something unexpected demanding your attention.

That’s why you need a little forethought.

And this week, I want you to do two things.

First, pick one physical activity you actually enjoy.

Walking, hiking, cycling, basketball, dancing, whatever gets you moving.

Second, put it on your calendar.

The same way you’d schedule anything else that matters.

Then show up for it.

Even if you only have 15 or 20 minutes.

Let “just moving” count.

Now, structured exercise and strength training still matter. They provide benefits that everyday movement alone may not fully address.

But don’t let the absence of a perfect workout become your excuse for doing nothing.

I wrote before that a six-pack isn’t unattainable if you use a little forethought.

This is the same principle applied to something much bigger.

Your quality of life.

If your approach fits your life and you can repeat it consistently, you’ve created a foundation you can build on.

And as your circumstances change, your approach can change with them.

Because if not, are you even living?
